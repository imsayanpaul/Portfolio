There is an idea in my journal that I keep coming back to: life as a mirror.

The way I see myself affects how I approach the world. If I decide that I am incapable before I begin, even a small setback can feel like proof. If I approach the same task as something I can learn, that setback becomes a problem I can work on.

My original notes called this **“The Four Mirror Laws of Nature.”** I find them more useful as a metaphor than as literal laws: four prompts for looking at the relationship between what I believe, what I notice, and what I actually do. Thoughts alone cannot guarantee an outcome. But they are worth examining when they keep shaping the choices I make.

## 1. Look at the image you bring to the mirror

The first idea concerns two things: **your view of yourself** and **your view of the world**.

“I am not good enough” sounds like a description, but it can quietly become a rule. It might stop you from applying for a role, sharing unfinished work, or asking a question. Then the lack of progress seems to confirm the original belief.

The same thing can happen with a view of the world. “There is never a good time to begin” leaves you waiting for conditions that may never arrive. There will always be something uncertain about a new project.

For me, the useful question is: **what kind of person do I want to practise being?**

Someone curious enough to ask. Someone patient enough to learn. Someone who finishes small things instead of waiting to feel ready for a big one.

That gives an identity something concrete to stand on. “I am a person who learns” can become opening the documentation, trying an unfamiliar approach, or admitting that I need help. It is a belief I can support with an action today.

The exercise from my notes is simple: write one sentence about who you want to become, and another about how you want to meet the world.

> I can learn things that feel difficult at first. There are opportunities worth looking for, even when the outcome is uncertain.

Neither sentence promises an easy life. Both leave room to move.

## 2. Give change time to become visible

You can imagine a different future in a moment. Building it takes longer.

This is the second mirror idea: **the reflection arrives with a delay**.

Learning a skill, building a product, or developing a habit involves work that may not look impressive while it is happening. One focused afternoon does not make someone an expert. A few quiet weeks do not necessarily mean an effort is pointless either.

The temptation is to treat today's result as a final verdict. I would rather ask whether the work is moving in a useful direction.

For a developer, that might mean understanding a bug that was confusing last week, writing a clearer API, or finishing a feature without abandoning it halfway through. Those are changes worth noticing before there is a bigger result to show.

Patience also needs feedback. If an approach keeps failing, it deserves another look. Staying committed to a direction can include changing the method.

**Give the work time, and keep checking what it is teaching you.**

## 3. Turn dissatisfaction into a direction

My third note was about the content of our attention: how much time we spend describing what we do not want.

“I hate being stuck.” “Everyone else is ahead.” “Nothing I make is good enough.”

Those feelings can be real. But repeating them does not tell me what to do next. A complaint becomes more useful when I can turn it into a specific question.

| When the thought is… | A question to work with |
| --- | --- |
| My projects are never good enough. | What is one part I can improve or finish this week? |
| Everyone else knows more than me. | What do I want to understand next? |
| I keep wasting time. | What is one distraction I can remove for the next hour? |
| I have made no progress. | What can I do now that I could not do a month ago? |

I also like the reminder to notice what is already there: skills, support, completed work, and the small decisions that went well. Ambition does not require dismissing all of that.

This is not an explanation for why some people struggle more than others. Circumstances, resources, and luck matter. The part I can use is narrower: when I have some room to act, I want my attention to help me find it.

## 4. Interrupt the loop with a deliberate choice

The last idea ties the others together.

One loop begins with a disappointing result. I use it to judge myself, approach the next attempt with the same expectation, and take the next disappointment as confirmation.

**Setback → fixed judgment → familiar response → another setback.**

A different loop starts by choosing a response before turning the result into an identity:

**Choose a direction → take a small action → observe honestly → adjust and repeat.**

Imagine abandoning a project because one feature is harder than expected. “I never finish anything” closes the conversation. “What is the smallest working version of this?” gives it somewhere to go.

The difficulty is still there. So is a choice about the next step.

That is the part of the mirror metaphor I want to keep: I do not have to let the current reflection decide every move I make next.

## A small practice to take away

There is no need to rebuild your entire worldview tonight. Start with one recurring thought and ask:

1. **What am I assuming about myself?** Write it down plainly.
2. **What would be a fairer, more useful belief?** Choose something you can act on.
3. **What is one action that supports it?** Make it small enough to do today.
4. **What will I review later?** Look for evidence, including what needs to change.

I cannot choose every circumstance or predict every result. I can pay closer attention to the story I bring into a situation—and whether my next action has to follow it.

Sometimes that is enough to make a different start.
